How to Verify a Supplement's Testing Lab Is Real

"Independently tested by a third-party laboratory" is one of the most reassuring claims a supplement brand can make. It is also one of the easiest to fake. A brand can name a laboratory that does not exist, cite a certificate it never received, or use a real laboratory's name without its knowledge. Checking whether the lab is real — and whether it actually tested this product — takes only a few minutes, and it is one of the most valuable checks a buyer can do.

Start with the name. A real laboratory has a public presence: a website, a physical address, a phone number, and a history. Search for the lab's name and look at what comes up. A genuine lab will have its own site describing its services, its accreditations, and its clients. A lab that exists only as a name on a supplement brand's website — with no website of its own, no address, and no trace anywhere else — is a warning sign. Also check whether the lab's name is suspiciously similar to a well-known lab's name: a one-letter difference from a reputable laboratory is a classic trick.

Next, check the accreditation. The most widely recognised standard for testing laboratories is ISO/IEC 17025, which means the lab's methods, equipment and staff have been independently assessed. Accreditation bodies publish lists of the laboratories they have accredited, and these lists are searchable. If a brand claims its lab is ISO 17025 accredited, look the lab up on the accreditor's website: the lab should appear, with the scope of its accreditation — which tests it is accredited to perform. A lab accredited for food microbiology is not necessarily accredited to test supplement potency, and the scope matters as much as the certificate.

Then check the certificate itself. A genuine certificate of analysis has specific features: the name of the product and the batch or lot number it refers to, the date of testing, the tests performed and their results, the limits the results were judged against, and the name and signature of the laboratory. A certificate with no batch number, no date, or no product name could belong to any product — or to none. Ask the brand for the certificate for the batch you are holding, and compare the lot number on your bottle with the lot number on the document. If they do not match, the certificate proves nothing about your bottle.

Fourth, consider contacting the lab directly. Most genuine laboratories will confirm, at least in general terms, whether they work with a particular brand. You do not need to ask for confidential results — simply ask whether the brand is a client and whether the certificate you hold is genuine. A lab that confirms the relationship is a strong signal; a lab that has never heard of the brand is a decisive one. Some labs also publish verification tools on their websites, where you can enter a certificate number and check it against their records.

Finally, remember what a lab test does and does not prove. A test proves that a specific batch contained what it claimed, without unacceptable contamination, at the time it was tested. It does not prove that the product works, that the dose is right, or that the next batch will be the same. Testing is a floor, not a ceiling — but it is a floor worth checking, because the brands that fake their testing are usually cutting corners everywhere else too.
